**Baba Ji (1/5): The First Step**

Every morning before we get up, we toss and turn at least once in our bed in hope of getting back to sleep. In addition to poor sleep hygiene, lacking an action plan for the day tends to aggravate this count. Even if we don’t have any action plan, we believe we’ll figure it out as the day passes and we finally leave bed. In the absence of any foreseen obstacles, waking up to a day is no gargantuan task, you see. But a few years ago, when the pandemic had not yet hit us so badly, I had to prepare myself for this uphill battle. Even though the days were sunny and nature was preparing itself for the flush, there was nothing remarkable about those days for me, as I was endeavouring to find my ikigai. Well, it was the beginning of my prefinal year at medical school and here I was, with no direction at all. Normally, our body produces a stress hormone called cortisol to deal with situations where it feels that it is under attack. But during those days, it felt as if my body was braving a perpetual fight-or-flight response and that my adrenal glands, situated on top of my kidneys, were working 24x7 to produce this hormone. Just like insulin resistance, I wonder if this could also have led to cortisol resistance in my body.

For most of us, experiencing existential crisis has been the cornerstone of growing up, forming opinions, making choices and finding some clarity in life. Not many realise the significance that negativity holds in our lives and what effort it takes to channel those never-ending, pop-up thoughts into hours-long workout sessions, introspection, Headspace and sometimes – just overthinking – overthinking about the purpose of life. Never in my life until this time had it occurred to me what life truly means to my uncle who lives in a village and has never left the place. Obsessed with woodworking, he has built his own house, which he keeps refurbishing time after time. Apart from this, I have seen him in the fields, growing crops to sustain his needs perennially. Sustenance? Is that all we need? Only survive and nothing more?

In a quest to find the origin of these endless thoughts, I started reading about Cognitive Neuroscience (VSI: Cognitive Neuroscience by Richard Passingham). I knew I had to heal my thought process, so I’d consciously play certain scripts of affirmations and echo them whilst my commute. Sometimes, I’d try hard to be mindful of my surroundings. One such day, on the way back to my hostel after the early morning lectures, there was the usual crowd visiting the hospital – some people walking faster than me, some staring at my white coat, some giving me the way to go first and a few people standing next to a person who was lying on the ground. Wait what? Someone is lying on the ground and I just moved past him? Should I turn around to help him or continue walking to the hostel? Anyhow, people are going to help him. Or are they? Why does his face look so familiar to me? Instantly, I had a flashback.

💭 It was my clinical posting in ENT, and I was indifferent to various topics except for vertigo. One of my batchmates was presenting the case of CSOM (Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media) which happens to be a long-standing middle ear infection. Our professor was attending to patients and simultaneously, he demonstrated cases so we could have a better understanding of the infection. After a while, there was a knock on the door that startled everybody. A lean man with a grey beard and waist-length dreadlocks stood on the door, not a rockstar but a sage. He had come to seek follow-up care for a wound over his left cheek. Because the cheekbone had partially eroded, the wound was gaping and hollow, and I could see into his maxillary sinus (the space behind our cheeks).

Within a few seconds I realised why the face looked familiar. The next moment, I was sitting beside him, interrogating and evaluating him for medical help. He was opening his eyes but not speaking. He gave me a gesture that he wanted to write, so I gave him a pen and a piece of paper. All I could understand from his writing was that he was an indoor patient in the hospital and was going back, but had fallen in the lane several times. There was probably some balance issue. Could it be vertigo? I, along with a few of my batchmates, managed to take him back to the hospital where he had been admitted. By this time, we were already late for our clinical posting. So we decided to brief the nurse about the incident.

In the afternoon, my friend and I went to see him, and I noticed that he had written a page explaining how grateful he was and also describing that currently, he had no money for the operation. I wondered what could have been the issue with his speech. I asked the nurse about the writing when she told me,“*Baba Ji has taken a vow of silence for the rest of his life.*”


**Baba Ji (2/5): An Antidote to Apathy**

*……a vow of silence for the rest of his life.*”

Was it just a hearsay or for real? It took me a few minutes to imbibe what the nurse had just told me. Although, the idea of eternal vow of silence shouldn’t have come as a surprise to me as I have grown up listening and reading stories of sages, their vows and sinister curses. But for Baba Ji, that would have meant adopting frugal lifestyle for the rest of his life with extreme discipline. Well, Baba Ji definitely appeared to be a sage. Yet, just to be sure, I went up to him for some paper-pen conversation.

As far as I remember, Baba Ji belonged to Rajasthan (a state in India) and had a family there. When he was in his forties, he made a decision to withdraw himself from this illusive world. He became a sage, a staunch devotee of Lord Shiva. Ever since, he had been travelling with the vow intact and here he was in his sixties, alone, all by himself. His reports revealed that he was suffering from left-sided Maxillary Carcinoma, a malignant cancer behind the cheek. Consequently, he had a wound over his left cheek for which he was being treated.

After this conversation, it was crystal clear that he was one of those sages from the stories that I spent my childhood listening and reading to. But at the same time, it was absolutely uncanny, the way things were unfolding, one after the other. Had I not stopped by to check on him that morning, would he have been lying on the sidewalk all day long? Because clearly, he wasn’t going to say anything. What makes this picture even more miserable for me is his being there all alone with that terminal illness.

Human physiology is such, that as soon as it senses danger, it performs an action that we know as a Reflex action. Now, is it possible that Baba Ji had mastered the science behind controlling these natural reflexes? Because, how can a person be so dedicated towards a vow, that for him, it is worth more than his own life? Even today, I don’t think I can imagine the extent a sage can go to, in order to escape this worldly miasma or what we commonly call as Saansarik Maya (सांसारिक माया).

This intrigued me even more because, on the other hand, I lacked motivation. I experienced this apathetic state of mind, where in I didn’t feel enthusiastic about anything. I didn’t care enough and I’d be least interested in whatever was happening around me. This might feel liberating but actually, it’s not. Apathy builds up distress and there is a growing sense of discontent. Being a doctor in making, the only thing that bothered me about my debilitating mental state was my absent emotional responsiveness. There were times in the emergency room when a nearly dying person would come with a sobbing chaperone but I wouldn’t feel a thing. And let me break it to you that no doctor is ever conditioned to feel nothing in such situations.

I knew certainly that it was existential depression but I wasn’t courageous enough like Baba Ji, to do something about it. In fact, many times I thought of dropping out of my medical school.

On several occasions, I would see him roaming in the hospital, alone, without getting helped by anyone. He would decline my assistance, when I ran to him offering some. Instead, he asked me to carry on with my work. My friend and I visited him, whenever we could spare some time. We got him fruits and orange candies, his favourite ones. He shared everything with us that we got for him except those orange candies, obviously. He just loved them. He was lean and didn’t like the food provided by the hospital. He said it was tasteless and he wanted pickle to spice it up. The next time we visited him, we saw a jar full of pickle beside his bed. With time, he gained some weight and got into better shape, and I too found a tremendously powerful antidote for my apathy. That antidote was empathy.


**Baba Ji (3/5): New Look (my perspective)**

Despite being eminently empathetic as a child, I had been hibernating emotionally for a while now. Even assisting Baba ji that day, in my perspective, was not an act driven by empathy. It was also not associated with the gratification people usually experience when they help somebody. Then why did I do it? It would be like keeping a loose end if I end this here. But do I have an answer to this question?

Well, let me try. See, it was easier for me to continue walking, being oblivious to whatever was happening around me. But what steered me to make a different choice that day was the deeply rooted feeling of loneliness within me. In my previous blog, I introduced you to my fractured emotional state, which kept me aloof from my surroundings. In that state, it was reasonable to presume that nobody would understand me. I felt lonely to the point that I wished for no one else to suffer the same way. However, in reality, there must have been so many people enduring the same quarter-life crisis as me. The thought that a human fears loneliness and longs for companionship instilled deep within me. Grief-stricken, I also lost touch with nature because, in my head, I was busy battling my own lonesomeness.

I remember looking around that day. Seeing him alone on the sidewalk surfaced this horrifying sensation within me. Well, Baba Ji was not a lonely man for whom the world was his home. Indeed, I was the one who projected my own insecurities onto him at that moment and helped him. Had I not felt insecure, what else could have stimulated me to do this deed?

With that said, as days passed by, the seed of empathy, planted by loneliness, began germinating at a pace so steady I couldn’t realise. I tried visiting Baba Ji along with my friend as often as I could, but exams and vacations caused inconsistency. Once, before my prefinal year exams, he wrote on a piece of paper that he had undergone two surgeries for this fast-growing lump over his left cheek (that leaves a wound after every excision) since the last time we met him. Now, he needed to undergo radiotherapy at a higher centre as the proliferation wouldn’t stop.

Did he even know what this fast-growing lump was? Was he aware of what cancer is and that it is a malignant one? I didn’t know for sure because I had never seen him cry or frown over his condition. On the contrary, there were several occasions when I visited him in a grumpy mood and exhibited worry. Nevertheless, he motivated us with his progressive thinking about the world. He used to read the newspaper. 😄

After a month-long vacation following the exams, we were back in the hostel. I wanted to meet Baba Ji and learn about the results of radiation therapy. To my surprise, I saw him right at the same place on the sidewalk where I first met him. He was helping a fruit seller with his sales. His new look left me spellbound insofar as I couldn’t believe my eyes. The mighty dreadlocks were all gone. Instead, he was wearing. a cap, a woollen sweater and trousers because winters were now harsh. His wound had recovered, and he was no longer a lean man. A few months ago, he was lying there in a miserable condition but now was a healthy man, lending his help to humankind.

What followed was a sense of contentment that I felt talking with him. He gestured to us that he was back now and the sessions of radiation therapy were over, following which he had to take a few cycles of chemotherapy. He was glowing with the happiness he felt in helping the fruit seller. He also offered us some fruits for free. But we were afraid the fruit seller would rebuke him. Rather, all this while he was smiling, seeing the bond Baba Ji made with every human being silently.

Besides, I thought I would not see him again as he had to go to the higher centre for treatment. Meeting him again would have sufficed. But his new look was like icing on the cake that was ample to fade my year-long hopelessness away that day. The unmeasurable joy and sense of hope associated with that day still prevail as I write this blog.


**Baba Ji (4/5) : Your Growth – Your Responsibility**

Looking back to when I started med school, I hoped that over the years I’d figure out what I want to do. But that’s not what you hope for from a professional course like MBBS, which demands your time, dedication and emotional wellbeing. I knew I was falling behind in grades, and it weighed heavily on me because I was an academic achiever for the major part of my life. I was surrounded by some of the most hardworking and committed future doctors, each with an inspiring story to tell. And there I was, quietly wondering if I even deserved to be among them. I’d be lying if I said that I didn’t like what I was learning, but I wanted to unfold the only passion of my life. I was living various facets of existentialism and most of it sucked.

After running into Baba Ji that day I sat there for a while feeling a quiet stillness settle in. Looking at the people passing by and then at the sidewalk, I thought to myself that besides me, it was only the sidewalk which had once embraced a helpless and debilitated man who was now well-kempt, nourished, and lending his hand to those around him. Today the same sidewalk, once ornamented with canopied trees on one side, was stripped bare and lined with nothing but amputated stumps. With every passing person, it slowly sank in how fleeting time truly is. They call it mono no aware in Japan. The idea is to accept the impermanence of everything, good or bad, existing today and that makes you live each and every moment more consciously. It’s like the quiet feeling that lingers when your favourite festival ends but all you can do is wait for the next year to celebrate it with even more heart. It became clear to my tense mind that I had to live with this awareness.

From then onwards, I spent more time with myself, knowing what made me anxious, vulnerable, content and more importantly, normal. I initiated an endless exploration of a person hidden inside me for many years. I started organising my stuff in the room and marked the days for doing laundry, grocery shopping and outings. I fixed my dietary plan, cooked the first meal to get the day going and filled my water bottle. Even on Sundays, I would wake up at the same time, comb my hair and plan the upcoming week. Two hobbies I picked up back then, which have stayed with me to this day, are reading and programming. The therapeutic effect that all these little activities had on my mind, was profound. The freedom I felt while doing them is timeless and it still finds me when I am lost in the world of existentialism. It took some time, but I accepted who I was becoming now; more aware. Even today, I won’t say I understand myself completely, but I’m open to the lifelong journey of discovering who I am.

For the next few days, I saw Baba Ji at the sidewalk, selling fruits. Once, my friend and I decided to check on him in the hospital. To our dismay, the cancerous lump over the left cheek was back. This time it also invaded the left eye, hindering vision significantly. I could tell this wasn’t going to end well. He had written a page-long note expressing his agony to us, as if he knew we would come visit him.

***“ Dear doctor, Jai Shankar! Kindly provide some treatment. This lump has come up to the eye. Whether it is necessary to operate, check after looking at the X-ray, or check it on mobile and proceed with the operation. Sometimes, the urge to rebel arises within me, but then I remind myself that I must accept whatever consequences my actions may bring. I must face them with courage and live life like a flower, always full of happiness. One must never be sad; this is what I say to my soul. You please convey this message to Dr. Sahab on my behalf. May you remain evergreen. Sorrow and happiness come and go; there’s no need to fear them. There’s no worry about death. Jai Shankar!”***

After reading this, I didn’t know what to say to him. Even in so much pain, he showed that it was his responsibility to face it. I kept a gentle smile to bring him hope, and he smiled back, giving his blessings. I was upset, but I knew the ENT specialist who was treating Baba Ji. He was the most empathetic and benevolent person with exceptional abilities in his field. He had been my teacher and an amazing mentor. I remember talking with him once about his condition when he told me that the prognosis was not good, but he would treat him for as long as it was possible. Knowing he was being properly looked after eased my worries.

I came back to my room and continued my exploration vigorously. Learning that my growth was my responsibility, I decided to step out of my cocoon and enrolled in a conference in Bangalore, which was going to be held in two months. Before the trip, I visited Baba Ji. He had a large antiseptic dressing covering the left side of his face, with a subtle smile on it. Yes, you’re thinking right! The tumour had been operated on, and Baba Ji was doing well. We talked for a while and before leaving, I took a picture of us. I submitted it to a photography competition at the Bangalore conference and captioned it ‘Making India Healthy’.


**Baba Ji (5/5) : Acceptance**

I took my first ever flight to Bangalore. With all the excitement in my heart, I finally made it to the conference. I had some amazing conversations with doctors whose work intrigued me to my very last neuron. I came across new advances in the field of medicine, particularly related to Diabetes Mellitus and Hypertension. I even won a cash prize for securing third place, where I had submitted the photo with Baba Ji under the theme ‘Making India Healthy’. I was thrilled by the response it received. I also decided to buy something for Baba Ji with that prize money later. Enriching me both personally and professionally, this trip was one hell of a journey. However, this blog isn’t really about the trip at all. It is about the truth I’m finally ready to tell myself today

I flew back less than a week later, visited my hometown, and finally, you could find me in my hostel room, sharing my experiences with my friend. I had obviously planned to meet Baba Ji that week, but my studies were keeping me worried as the final exams were nearing. Despite everything, a few days later I dropped in on him. As soon as I went up to greet him, he woke up from his nap and turned to find me there. As he turned to face me, I froze in place. I couldn’t comprehend what I was looking at. Apart from his usual smile, which had taken a back seat, something far more precious was gone forever. I tried speaking but the words just wouldn’t come out so I stood there.

Terrified, he moved his hands, trying to explain what had happened. He gestured that a couple of days ago, the dressing on the left side of his face was finally removed. He stood up and looked at himself in the mirror. He couldn’t recognise the face staring back. An unfamiliar reflection, with a scar on the left cheek and an absent left eye, was now his new identity. Shaken to the very core, I gathered my wits to talk to him. I tried to reassure him. I don’t know how liberating it really was for him because I needed some time to process for myself what had just happened. I asked him if he knew about the eye surgery beforehand. He replied that he had known about it all along and that the removal had always been part of the plan. He was informed about the cancer growth involving the left eye and its consequences if not operated on in time.

I couldn’t get his image out of my mind when I returned to the hostel. I felt weak. I had never seen him like that before. I did open up to some of my friends and then completed my assignments. In a few days, I met him again. With contentment on his face, he wrote that one day, when he’s fine and leaves this hospital, he will go to Manimahesh and live there for the rest of his life. I was relieved to see him composed again, with the strength only he could find in times like these. The preparedness to live life again was written all over his face. There was something that kept him going, a hope and a will to live even after a few bad days.

I think I was beginning to understand my life. I realized that there are times so rough and scary that you can’t understand the whys of your life. You feel anxious, unsettled and confused. But meaning finds its way to you eventually. And during that time you prepare for life. You work hard on yourself. You embrace failures. You care for yourself. You value family and friends. You show up, you grow. You don’t stop trying till you find YOU.

I got busy with the academics and the maternity postings. I wasn’t resting well. My old patterns were slipping in, numbing me day after day. I saw them clearly this time and that loosened some of their hold on me. I started preparing myself. Some days seemed worse than the last time, but only for a moment. I’d talk to my counsellor, exercise, eat healthy, sleep and most importantly take it one day at a time.

One day I realised it had been several weeks, and I hadn’t checked on Baba Ji. So I went up to him. I didn’t find him in his bed. It wasn’t unusual, as he was fond of strolling in the hospital. I couldn’t trace any of his possessions near his bed. Now that was odd! Was he shifted to the radiotherapy ward? I went up to the nurse to look at his file. She looked at me, paused for a moment, then replied,

*“The one who never spoke?”*

*“Yes!”*

*“Why do you ask?”*

*“I just wanted to see what the current treatment plan is. By the way, where is he?”*

*“Oh! He passed away about a month ago. He was put on chemotherapy, which didn’t suit him. Poor Baba Ji! He became very weak from those unstoppable bouts of vomiting. You couldn’t have seen his condition. He had two episodes of seizures in the radiotherapy ward, and the last one was the one he couldn’t survive. I think the cancer had reached his brain.”*

*“…….uhmmm do we have his file here?”*

*“I don’t think so. Must be in the record section now.”*

*“Okay! Thank you so much.”*

I almost ran out of the hospital while calling my friend who used to visit him too.

*“Hey! Baba Ji is…..”*

*“I know. It has been some time.”*

*“Why didn’t you tell me?”*

She said nothing and after a long silence, I hung up the phone.

I remember being on the other sidewalk, leaning slightly against the railings, my brain frozen, not knowing what I was even looking at. Too tired to think, too hollow to cry, I drifted into silence. After a few minutes, knowing some things have changed forever, I called my counsellor. The voice could barely come out of me,

*“Do you remember the prize money?”*

*“Yes, what about it?”*

*“It’s too late now.”* I cried.

*“I’m really sorry.”*

*“One month is a long time, right? How bad is it that I didn’t give a thought to meeting him all this time?”*

*“But you know, you met him enough times to give him some of his best days here. He did the same for you both.”*

**It has been five years now. Sometimes, my mind tries to trick me into believing that Baba ji left the hospital, climbed to Manimahesh and is living there. Because I didn’t see him go in front of my eyes, I have no proof of his demise. It’s truly hard to find closure when you miss the funeral of someone close. The grieving process is disrupted. The only other way I could find for myself was writing this blog series. Finding words to express the emotions and feelings I experienced in those moments, helps me surface the suppressed despair, acknowledge it and then become comfortable with it. It helps me find the part of myself that was once concealed in those moments.**
